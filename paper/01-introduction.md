When students sit a high-stakes reading examination, the texts they meet should not differ in kind from the texts their schooling has given them. Where the examination is set by the same state that provides the school's materials, the expectation is stronger. Whether or not the two are formally linked, a public school system that sends its students to a national examination takes on some responsibility for preparing them to read what that examination asks them to read. This paper asks whether the reading texts in the state English coursebooks of Greek upper-secondary schools match, in their linguistic demands, the reading texts of the English paper in the Panhellenic university entrance examinations.

Comparisons of this kind have been made in several national systems: Japan (Underwood, 2010), Taiwan (Tai & Chen, 2015), Thailand (Srisunakrua & Chumworatayee, 2019), Iran (Mirshojaee & Sahragard, 2015), South Korea (Lee & Kang, 2021) and Turkey (Gedik & Kolsal, 2022). They share a design: coursebook texts and examination texts are profiled with readability formulas or with a multi-index tool such as Coh-Metrix (McNamara et al., 2014), and the profiles are compared. Several report that the examination texts are harder (Gedik & Kolsal, 2022; Lee & Kang, 2021; Mirshojaee & Sahragard, 2015; Srisunakrua & Chumworatayee, 2019). Three features of this work limit what it can show, however. First, a failure to find a difference is usually read as evidence that the texts are alike, although no test of equivalence is made. Second, many indices are compared at once with little control of error rates, and the classic formulas they lean on capture word and sentence length rather than the kinds of difficulty that L2 reading research has identified (Crossley et al., 2008, 2011). Third, the studies describe *that* the two sets of texts differ but rarely ask *why*: whether the differences reflect the kinds of text each set contains or the way texts of the same kind are written.

## The Greek Context

In Greece, admission to state universities depends on the Panhellenic examinations, sat at the end of the third year of the General Lyceum, the upper-secondary school. These examinations shape upper-secondary teaching strongly, both in schools and in the private tuition centres that many candidates attend (Tsiplakou & Tsagari, 2023). English is a compulsory subject in all three years of the Lyceum, and every student studies it. In the Panhellenic examinations, English is a special subject, taken by candidates who apply to the departments that require it (Ministry of Education, Religious Affairs and Sports, 2024, Art. 1). Since 2010, each English paper has opened with a reading passage, now specified as an authentic text of about 330–370 words, followed by eight to ten comprehension questions, language-awareness tasks and a writing task (Ministry of Education, Religious Affairs and Sports, 2024, Art. 2). <!-- source: ΦΕΚ 7261/Β/2024, Art. 2 -->

The English taught in the Lyceum is based on two state coursebooks: *English 1* for the first year and *English 2* for the second and third years (Manolopoulou-Sergi, Papachristou, Gkouvatsou, et al., 2019; Manolopoulou-Sergi, Papachristou, Poulla, et al., 2019). Both were in use throughout the period covered here, and both are freely available online, as are all past examination papers. The Panhellenic English paper is not formally tied to these books, and many candidates also prepare for it in private tuition centres, as is common for the Panhellenic examinations generally (Tsiplakou & Tsagari, 2023). But the books are the reading material that the state itself provides to every student who might later sit the examination. If the examination's texts make demands of a different kind from the books', students who rely on the school alone will not have met that kind of text in the material the school provides.

An earlier study of the same materials ([Author, 2023]) compared the coursebook texts with 13 examination passages (2010–2022) on Coh-Metrix indices and readability formulas. It found that the examination texts used more abstract vocabulary and fewer passive constructions, but no difference on the readability formulas. That analysis shares the limitations described above. It tested many indices with little correction, could not test whether the groups were equivalent where they did not differ, and relied on a tool whose code is not open. It also left open whether the differences it found were a matter of genre: the coursebooks contain stories and biographies, while the examination passages appear to be expository or argumentative articles.

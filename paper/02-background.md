# Background

## Text difficulty and comprehensibility

How hard a text is to read depends on both the text and the reader. A reader's vocabulary, background knowledge, purpose and L2 proficiency all shape comprehension, and no measure of the text alone can predict how well a particular reader will understand it (Alderson, 2000; Grabe, 2009). Kintsch and Vipond (1979) made the distinction early: *readability* is a property of a text, while comprehension is an outcome of reading. Text measures are still useful, because the text is the part of the reading situation that materials writers and examiners control. A coursebook can be compared with an examination on what its texts demand of a reader, even though it cannot be compared on what its readers achieve. This study makes that text-side comparison and only that.

## Readability formulas and their limits

The classic readability formulas estimate difficulty from word length (in syllables or letters) and sentence length. Flesch Reading Ease (Flesch, 1948) and the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level (Kincaid et al., 1975) are still the most widely used, and many of the alignment studies reviewed below rely on them. For L2 readers they have long been criticised. Carrell (1987) argued that they ignore the features that matter most for L2 reading, such as background knowledge and the organisation of the text. Brown (1998) found that L1 formulas predicted the difficulty of cloze passages for Japanese EFL students only weakly, and he proposed an EFL-specific index; later attempts to reproduce it gave mixed results (Greenfield, 2004). The alternative has been to model the cognitive processes that reading involves. Coh-Metrix (Graesser et al., 2004; McNamara et al., 2014) computes over a hundred indices of a text at several levels: words, syntax, the text base, and the situation model. Crossley et al. (2008) built an L2 readability index from three of them: word frequency, syntactic similarity between sentences, and content-word overlap. It predicted EFL cloze scores better than the classic formulas, and a later study found that it also tracked intuitive simplification more closely (Crossley et al., 2011). More recent models combine many such features (Crossley, Skalicky, & Dascalu, 2019; Crossley et al., 2023), and eye-tracking evidence suggests that they predict L2 readers' processing effort better than the classic formulas do (Nahatame, 2021). Across this work, three dimensions recur: the vocabulary of a text, its syntax, and its cohesion, meaning how explicitly its sentences are linked (Graesser et al., 2011).

Two practical problems follow for a study like this one. The first is that multi-index tools produce many correlated indices. Comparing two small groups of texts on dozens of them invites false positives unless the comparisons are limited and corrected. The second is that Coh-Metrix is a web-based tool whose code is not open, so its results cannot be fully reproduced or inspected. Open tools now cover most of what it measures: TAACO for cohesion (Crossley et al., 2016; Crossley, Kyle, & Dascalu, 2019), syntactic complexity analysers (Lu, 2010) and published word norms (Brysbaert et al., 2014; Scott et al., 2019).

## Vocabulary: frequency, coverage and thresholds

Vocabulary knowledge is consistently among the strongest predictors of L2 reading comprehension (Grabe, 2009). The most direct way to relate a text's vocabulary to a reader's is *lexical coverage*: the percentage of a text's running words that the reader knows. Hu and Nation (2000) found that comprehension of a fiction text rose with coverage, and that most readers needed about 98% coverage to understand it adequately without help. Laufer and Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) proposed two thresholds: 95% coverage for minimal comprehension and 98% for optimal comprehension. Nation (2006) estimated, from frequency-ranked word-family lists, that reaching 98% coverage of written English requires 8,000–9,000 word families. <!-- source: Nation (2006), cited figure -->

The same lists can describe a text rather than a reader: the frequency band by which a text reaches 95% or 98% coverage is an estimate of the vocabulary size that it assumes. The thresholds should not be read as sharp cut-offs, however. Comprehension rises gradually with coverage, and estimates depend on the text, the test and the reader (Cobb, 2016; Webb, 2021). Coverage profiles have been used to examine examination texts directly. Yıldız (2023), for instance, profiled the reading passages of Turkey's university-level English examinations against the 95% and 98% thresholds.

Frequency is not the only lexical property that matters. Words also differ in how concrete they are: how far they refer to things that can be seen, heard or touched. Concrete texts are rated as easier to understand and are better remembered than abstract texts on the same topic (Sadoski et al., 1993). Concreteness and frequency are related but distinct, since common words can be abstract (*idea*, *reason*, *way*). Large-scale norms now give concreteness ratings for tens of thousands of English words (Brysbaert et al., 2014), alongside related properties such as imageability and familiarity (Scott et al., 2019).

## Genre

Texts of different genres make different demands. Narratives follow a familiar temporal and causal structure, recount concrete events and characters, and draw on everyday knowledge. Expository texts organise information by logical relations such as cause, comparison and problem–solution, and often deal with unfamiliar and abstract content. A meta-analysis of L1 studies found that narrative texts are, on average, better comprehended and recalled than expository texts (Mar et al., 2021). Coh-Metrix treats narrativity as one of the main dimensions along which texts vary (Graesser et al., 2011; McNamara et al., 2014). Genre matters for coursebook–examination comparisons in two ways. First, if the two sets of texts differ in genre mix, differences on text measures may reflect genre rather than difficulty within a genre. Second, the implications differ. A difference that genre explains suggests that the coursebook should include more texts of the examination's kind. A difference that persists within a genre suggests that the coursebook's texts of that kind are themselves unlike the examination's.

## Coursebooks and examinations

Several studies have compared the reading texts of national coursebooks with those of the examinations that follow them. Underwood (2010) compared the reading texts of Japanese senior-high-school textbooks approved by the Ministry of Education (MEXT) with those of the National Center Test for university entrance. Tai and Chen (2015) compared Taiwanese junior-high textbooks with the senior-high entrance examination. Srisunakrua and Chumworatayee (2019) compared Thai textbooks with the national O-NET test using readability formulas. Mirshojaee and Sahragard (2015) compared Iranian general English textbooks with MA entrance examinations on vocabulary, syntax and discourse measures and reported a sharp mismatch. Lee and Kang (2021) used Coh-Metrix to compare Korean senior-high textbooks with the College Scholastic Ability Test, and Gedik and Kolsal (2022) made a corpus-based comparison of Turkish textbooks with the university entrance examinations. Chen (2016) showed that difficulty does not always progress as intended even within a single coursebook series. [CHECK: confirm the findings attributed to each study against the full texts.]

These studies are motivated by a concern with alignment between what is taught and what is tested, a concern sharpened by the fact that high-stakes examinations shape teaching (Alderson & Wall, 1993; Cheng et al., 2004). In Greece, the Panhellenic examinations are known to shape upper-secondary teaching (Tsiplakou & Tsagari, 2023). Yet no published study has compared the Lyceum English coursebooks with the Panhellenic English examination, apart from the earlier analysis of the present corpus ([Author, 2023]).

The present study departs from this body of work in four ways. It uses only open, reproducible measures, validated against Coh-Metrix where possible. It fixes a small number of primary measures in advance and corrects for multiple comparisons. It tests the central lexical comparison for equivalence, so that "no difference" can be distinguished from "not enough evidence". And it asks whether genre accounts for the differences it finds.

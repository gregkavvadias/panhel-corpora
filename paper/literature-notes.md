## Coursebook–exam comparisons (the gap the paper fills)

- **Underwood (2010):** Japan; MEXT reading textbooks vs the National Center Test. This is the closest design model. [CHECK: direction of the readability gap]
- **Lee & Kang (2021):** Korea; senior-high textbooks vs the CSAT, Coh-Metrix indices.
- **Gedik & Kolsal (2022):** Turkey; high-school textbooks vs the university entrance exams, corpus-based.
- **Tai & Chen (2015):** Taiwan; junior-high textbooks vs the entrance exam. Short proceedings paper.
- **Srisunakrua & Chumworatayee (2019):** Thailand; textbooks vs O-NET, readability formulas.
- **Mirshojaee & Sahragard (2015):** Iran; general English books vs MA reading tests. It reports a sharp divergence in vocabulary, structure and discourse (from the abstract).
- **Yıldız (2023):** Turkey; lexical coverage of the YDS/YÖKDİL-type tests. Supports coverage as the lead lexical measure in an exam context.
- **Chen (2016):** difficulty progression within ELT textbook series. Relevant to the point that coursebooks are not uniform.
- **Common pattern:** most of these studies are single-country and descriptive, rely on classic formulas or proprietary Coh-Metrix, and do not test equivalence. None examines genre as an explanation. This is the paper's opening.

## Readability and text difficulty

- **Classic formulas:** Flesch (1948) and Kincaid et al. (1975). Criticism for L2: Carrell (1987), Brown (1998), Greenfield (2004), Fulcher (1997) and Crossley et al. (2011), who find that cognitively based indices outperform the classic formulas.
- **Cognitive and cohesion-based approaches:** Kintsch & Vipond (1979); Graesser et al. (2004, 2011); McNamara et al. (2014); Crossley et al. (2007, 2008, 2019); Nahatame (2021), who links readability to eye-tracking effort in L2; and CLEAR (Crossley et al., 2023).
- **Reading theory:** Alderson (2000) and Grabe (2009), for the reader–text interaction and the caveat that text measures are not comprehension.

## Vocabulary coverage

- **Thresholds:** Hu & Nation (2000) and Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) for the 95% minimum and 98% optimal coverage levels; Nation (2006) for the 8–9k families needed to reach 98% in written text. Greg's point that "more than 10% unknown makes the text harder" fits Hu & Nation's comprehension curve.
- **Critiques:** Cobb (2016) and Webb (2021) on the limits of the thresholds. This justifies reporting coverage curves, not a single cut-off.

## Measures and statistics

- **Lexical:** Brysbaert et al. (2014) for concreteness; Scott et al. (2019) for the Glasgow norms; McCarthy & Jarvis (2010) for MTLD and HD-D; Kyle & Crossley (2015) for lexical sophistication.
- **Syntactic:** Lu (2010) for MLC (L2SCA definition).
- **Cohesion:** Crossley et al. (2016, 2019) for TAACO.
- **Statistics:** Lakens (2017) for TOST; Benjamini & Hochberg (1995); Hedges (1981); Zou & Hastie (2005) for the elastic net.
- **Software:** Honnibal et al. (2020) for spaCy; Pedregosa et al. (2011) for scikit-learn; Nation (2017) for the BNC/COCA lists [CHECK].

## Genre and L2 reading (for RQ2)

- The literature is thin and mostly L1: narrative is generally easier than expository text for inferential comprehension. L2 evidence is mixed. The searches found no corpus-based L2 study that measures text difficulty by genre, which supports framing RQ2 as exploratory.
- **Not yet included:** the L1 meta-analysis (narrative vs expository inferencing) and the EFL narrative-vs-expository discourse-analysis study (System/ScienceDirect 1990). Bibliographic details were not verified, so add them only if the background section needs them and after Crossref verification.
- **Freedle & Kostin (1999):** text characteristics predict item difficulty in TOEFL. Supports the claim that the text itself matters in a test.

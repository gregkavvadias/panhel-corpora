# Conclusion

The reading texts of the Panhellenic English examination ask no more of a reader's vocabulary size than the texts of the state Lyceum coursebooks: by word frequency, the two are equivalent. They differ in kind. The examination texts are more abstract, use fewer passives, and pack more, shorter clauses into sentences that are more explicitly linked. Every examination passage is informational or argumentative, whereas nearly half of the coursebook texts are narratives. Yet genre does not explain the difference: the coursebooks' own expository texts are more concrete and more passive than the examination's. The readability formulas register none of this.

For the Greek state school, the implication is specific. Its coursebooks prepare students for texts of the right frequency but the wrong kind. Closing that gap means giving students discursive, abstract expository prose to read, not simply more difficult vocabulary or fewer stories. More generally, comparisons of coursebooks and examinations should test for equivalence where they find no difference, look beyond frequency and formulas, and ask whether differences lie between genres or within them.

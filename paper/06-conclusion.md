# Conclusion

The reading texts of the Panhellenic English examination ask no more of a reader's vocabulary size than the texts of the state Lyceum coursebooks: by word frequency, the two are equivalent. They differ in kind. The examination texts are more abstract and, descriptively, use fewer passives and more clauses per sentence. Every examination passage is informational or argumentative, whereas nearly half of the coursebook texts are narratives. Yet genre does not explain the difference: the coursebooks' own non-narrative texts are more concrete and use more passives than the examination's. The readability formulas register none of this.

For the Greek state school, the implication is specific. Its coursebooks offer texts that match the examination's in vocabulary frequency but not in kind. Closing that gap means including more abstract, discursive expository prose among the texts students read, not simply more difficult vocabulary or fewer stories. More generally, comparisons of coursebooks and examinations should test for equivalence where they find no difference, look beyond frequency and formulas, and ask whether differences lie between genres or within them.

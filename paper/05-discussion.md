# Discussion

## What the examination texts demand

The answer to RQ1 has three parts. First, by word frequency the examination texts are no harder than the coursebook texts. The two groups are equivalent in 5k coverage within a margin that the coverage literature would treat as negligible, and both groups reach the 95% threshold by the 5k level in all but one text. A student whose vocabulary is adequate for the coursebooks' texts would, on frequency alone, be equally well equipped for the examination's. Second, the examination texts are clearly more abstract. Their content words are less concrete and less imageable, and the difference is large (*g* above 1 in absolute value on both measures). It also holds in every subset, including the 13 passages that the earlier analysis did not see. Third, on syntax and cohesion the examination texts are not harder by the primary measures, and the descriptive measures describe a different kind of prose rather than a harder one. The examination's sentences contain more but shorter clauses and far fewer passives, and its sentences are linked more explicitly, through semantic overlap and connectives.

Taken together, the examination texts have the profile of expository and argumentative writing about ideas. Such writing is more abstract in vocabulary, more often makes claims and qualifies them in subordinate clauses, uses connectives to build an argument, and stays close to its topic from sentence to sentence. The coursebook texts are more concrete, more often passive, and less tightly linked. None of this registers on the readability formulas, which place the two groups close together. A teacher or materials writer who relied on Flesch scores would conclude that the coursebooks match the examination, and would miss the one large lexical difference this study finds.

The answer to RQ2 sharpens this picture. The two groups can be told apart reliably from the open measures, and the features that separate them are the same ones that RQ1 found: abstractness, clauses per sentence, passives and the measures of explicit linking. The groups also differ sharply in genre. Twelve of the 27 coursebook texts are narratives, while none of the 26 examination passages is. But genre does not explain the difference. When the narratives are set aside, the coursebooks' own informational and argumentative texts are still more concrete and far more passive than the examination's. Removing genre shrinks the concreteness gap by about a sixth and the passives gap hardly at all. The mismatch, then, lies less in how many narratives the coursebooks contain than in the kind of non-narrative prose they offer. The coursebooks' expository texts tend to describe and report: they are concrete, often passive, and centred on events, people and things. The examination's expository texts tend to discuss: they are abstract and active, and built on reasoning across clauses.

## Relation to earlier work

The finding that vocabulary frequency does not differ, while abstractness does, refines the usual conclusion of coursebook–examination comparisons that the examination texts are simply "harder". In this case they are not harder on the dimension that the most widely used lexical measures capture. The difference lies in a dimension that frequency-based and formula-based profiles do not measure. Studies that rely on readability formulas, or that read the absence of a significant difference as evidence of a match, may therefore miss mismatches of this kind. The equivalence test makes the distinction explicit: coverage is not merely "not significantly different", it is shown to be equivalent within a stated margin.

The results also bear on the earlier analysis of this corpus ([Author, 2023]). Its central observation, that the examination texts are more abstract and use fewer passives, holds with open measures, corrected statistics and twice as many examination texts, and is about as large in the 13 passages it did not include. What the present study adds is the equivalence in vocabulary frequency, which shows that the abstractness difference is not a by-product of rarer words, and the finding that the difference persists within genre.

## Implications for materials and teaching

The study measures texts, not students, so its implications concern what the coursebooks offer rather than what students achieve. Three follow.

First, adding vocabulary of a higher frequency band would not close the gap, because there is no frequency gap to close. The gap lies in the abstract, idea-centred vocabulary that discursive prose relies on, the words for claims, causes, attitudes and processes. Coursebook writers could select or adapt expository texts whose vocabulary is of similar frequency but more abstract.

Second, replacing narratives with expository texts would help less than it seems. The coursebooks' existing expository texts already differ from the examination's in the same direction as the narratives. What the coursebooks lack is a particular kind of expository text: the discursive, argued article, in an active voice, that develops a position across tightly linked sentences.

Third, teachers working with the current books can supplement them with texts of this kind, and can draw students' attention to how such texts are built: how abstract nouns carry an argument, how clauses qualify a claim, and how connectives and repeated content signal the argument's structure. Because the examination is not formally tied to the coursebooks, none of this implies that the books should become examination-preparation material. It implies only that students who rely on the books alone should meet, before the examination, the kind of prose that the examination asks them to read.

## Limitations

The study is text-side. It shows that the examination texts make different lexical and discourse demands, not that these demands make the texts harder for Greek candidates to comprehend. Evidence on that question would require data from readers, such as comprehension scores on texts of both kinds. The published examination statistics do not provide it, because they report scores for the whole paper rather than for the reading task. [CHECK: Greg to confirm the statistics cover 2019–2026 only.]

The corpus is small (27 and 26 texts) and comes from a single country and a single pair of coursebooks. The confidence intervals are correspondingly wide, and only large differences can be detected with confidence. Coverage is near the ceiling in every text, so the coverage measure has little room to vary; the equivalence result should be read in raw points, as stated, and not as evidence that the vocabulary is identical in every respect.

Some measures rest on narrower foundations than others. The Glasgow norms cover only about half of each text's content words, so imageability and familiarity are reported descriptively. Three open measures agree poorly with the Coh-Metrix indices they were meant to parallel (adjectives, causal connectives and semantic overlap), and four, including mean length of clause, have no counterpart in the earlier data. The clause rule misses a small share of finite clauses. The genre labels were proposed with AI assistance and confirmed by a single coder; the second coder's agreement on a sample ([κ: pending]) is the only reliability check. Finally, the permutation test used 200 permutations. Because no permuted run reached the observed AUC, more runs would lower the floor of the *p* value but could not change the conclusion.

## Further research

Two lines of work would extend these findings. The first is reader-side: testing whether Greek Lyceum students comprehend abstract, discursive expository texts less well than concrete, descriptive ones of matched frequency, and whether the gap narrows with targeted reading practice. The second is comparative: applying the same open, pre-specified procedure to the coursebook–examination pairs of other national systems would show whether the pattern found here — equivalent frequency, different abstractness, a mismatch within genre — is specific to Greece or more general.

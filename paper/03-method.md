# Method

## Corpus

The corpus has two groups of texts: the reading texts of the two state coursebooks for the General Lyceum and the main reading passages of the Panhellenic English examination (Table 1). It was first assembled for [Author, 2023], and the present study extends and re-analyses it. All texts, together with the scripts and the derived measures, are openly available at [anonymised repository link].

**Coursebook texts.** *English 1* is the state coursebook for English in the first year of the Lyceum, and *English 2* for the second and third years (Manolopoulou-Sergi, Papachristou, Gkouvatsou, et al., 2019; Manolopoulou-Sergi, Papachristou, Poulla, et al., 2019), so together they span the three years leading to the examination. Both were in use throughout the period covered by the examination texts, and both are freely available from the Greek state's digital textbook service. Like the examination passages, the coursebook texts are adapted from published sources rather than written for the books. I included every text designed for reading comprehension. Texts that exist only to present vocabulary were excluded, as were audio scripts. Several units have no reading text. One text that had been included in the earlier analysis, an *English 2* exercise in which learners restore the spaces and punctuation of a run-together passage, was excluded here because it is a formatting task rather than a reading passage. That leaves 27 coursebook texts, 10 from *English 1* and 17 from *English 2*.

**Examination texts.** Each Panhellenic English paper opens with a reading passage (Part A), followed by tasks on that passage and a writing task. I took only the Part A passage. The corpus holds all 17 June papers since the reading component was introduced (2010–2026) and the 9 September resit papers available on the Ministry's examination archive (2018–2026). Papers set for candidates from the Greek diaspora were excluded. They are a separate examination for a different population and are popularly believed to be easier, so including them could bias the examination group.

The 13 June passages from 2010 to 2022 come from the earlier corpus. The other 13 were extracted from the official PDF papers by script, keeping only the text visible on the printed page; one paper (September 2024) contained two hidden text layers, both removed (one automatically, one by a recorded correction after the extractor flagged it). I checked every extracted passage against its PDF page before it entered the corpus. The only other correction was an obvious typographical error in one paper.

The examination passages are short and uniform in length; the coursebook texts are longer and vary much more (Table 1). Because length can affect several of the measures, all measures were also computed on a length-matched version of the coursebook texts (see Robustness checks).

## Measures

I measured text difficulty along the three dimensions that the readability literature most consistently identifies: vocabulary, syntax and cohesion (Crossley et al., 2008; Graesser et al., 2011). For each dimension I chose one or two measures as primary and treated all others as descriptive. I also computed two classic readability formulas for reference. Every measure was computed with open tools, so that anyone can reproduce it from the texts. Full definitions are in Appendix A.

**Text preparation.** Texts were parsed with spaCy 3.8 (`en_core_web_sm`; Honnibal et al., 2020); the semantic overlap measure uses the word vectors of `en_core_web_md`. Vocabulary measures use the full text. Syntactic, cohesion and readability measures use the text without its title and subheadings, because headings parse as sentences and inflate counts of short sentences and noun phrases. Content words are alphabetic tokens tagged as nouns, verbs, adjectives or adverbs, excluding proper nouns.

**Vocabulary.** The primary lexical measure is *5k coverage*: the percentage of running words that belong to the 1k–5k word-family frequency bands of Nation's (2017) BNC/COCA lists, with the supplementary lists (for example, transparent compounds and abbreviations) counted as known. Proper nouns, which make up 6.6% of coursebook tokens and 1.1% of examination tokens, are excluded from the count; as a check, coverage was also computed with them counted as known. They were identified from Nation's proper-noun list and the parser's tags, and I reviewed every word classed as a proper noun by hand. Coverage was chosen because it links directly to the comprehension thresholds in the L2 reading literature: 95% coverage for minimal and 98% for optimal comprehension (Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski, 2010; see also Hu & Nation, 2000). I also report coverage at every band from 1k to 9k, and the band at which each group's mean reaches 95% and 98%.

The second primary lexical measure is *concreteness*: the mean Brysbaert et al. (2014) concreteness rating (1–5) of each text's content words, weighted by token. The norms covered at least 95% of the content words in every text. Descriptive vocabulary measures were imageability and familiarity from the Glasgow Norms (Scott et al., 2019), and lexical diversity measured by MTLD and HD-D (McCarthy & Jarvis, 2010; computed with the lexical-diversity package, Kyle, n.d.). The Glasgow Norms are smaller than the Brysbaert norms and cover only about half of each text's content words (59% in the coursebook texts and 54% in the examination texts on average), so imageability and familiarity are reported descriptively only.

**Syntax.** The primary syntactic measure is *mean length of clause* (MLC): words per finite clause, following the definition in Lu's (2010) L2 Syntactic Complexity Analyzer. Finite clauses were identified from the dependency parse (main, coordinate with their own subject, complement, adverbial and relative clauses with a finite verb). <!-- source: README clause-rule check -->A manual check of all 1,431 sentences in the corpus found that the rule misses about 1% of finite clauses, spread across both groups. Descriptive syntactic measures were clauses per sentence, mean sentence length, and the incidence per 1,000 words of passives, noun phrases, verbs, adjectives, prepositional phrases, negation, gerunds and infinitives.

**Cohesion.** The primary cohesion measure is *content-word overlap* between adjacent sentences, modelled on Coh-Metrix's CRFCWO1 index (McNamara et al., 2014): for each pair of adjacent sentences, the proportion of content words whose lemma occurs in both sentences, averaged over the text. Descriptive measures were semantic overlap between adjacent sentences (the cosine similarity of their mean word vectors, a stand-in for Coh-Metrix's latent semantic analysis), and the incidence of connectives and causal connectives from the TAACO lists (Crossley et al., 2016; Crossley, Kyle, & Dascalu, 2019).

**Readability formulas.** Flesch Reading Ease (Flesch, 1948) and the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level (Kincaid et al., 1975), with syllables counted by Pyphen (CourtBouillon, n.d.), are reported because they remain the measures most often used in practice.

**Validation.** The earlier analysis measured the original texts with Coh-Metrix 3.0 (McNamara et al., 2014), a web-based tool whose code is not open. Where a Coh-Metrix index corresponded to one of the open measures, I correlated the two (Spearman's ρ) on the 40 original texts that remain in the corpus, pooled and within each group. The within-group correlations are the stricter test, because a pooled correlation partly reflects the difference between the groups. Mean length of clause, clauses per sentence, MTLD and familiarity have no Coh-Metrix counterpart in the earlier data. Concreteness agreed closely with its Coh-Metrix counterpart (ρ = .90); content-word overlap agreed well only within the examination texts, and three descriptive measures (adjectives, causal connectives and semantic overlap) agreed poorly and are best read as related measures, not replacements. Full results are in Appendix B.

## What was fixed in advance

I use "pre-specified" in a narrow sense. The two lexical primary measures (5k coverage with its ±2-point margin, and concreteness) were fixed before they were first computed, on the original 13 examination texts, and then reapplied unchanged to all 26; those results had been seen before the four-measure family was set up. The other two primary measures, the four-test correction family and all other measure definitions were committed to the repository before any of them was computed, and no definition changed afterwards. The formatting exercise was excluded at that point, after the lexical results on the extended corpus had been seen, and the genre analysis was designed after the RQ1 results were known. The earlier analysis ([Author, 2023]) had already found the examination texts lower in concreteness and in passives; the present study re-tests those observations with open measures and corrected statistics. As a partial check on the 13 added examination texts, I report the primary tests for the original 13 papers and for the June papers alone.

## Analysis

**RQ1: group comparisons.** The four primary measures were compared with Welch's *t* test, which does not assume equal variances, with a Mann–Whitney test as a check. Effect sizes are Hedges' *g* (Hedges, 1981; examination minus coursebook), <!-- source: analysis settings (effects.py) -->with 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals from 5,000 resamples. The four primary *p* values form a single family, corrected with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). Descriptive measures are reported with effect sizes and intervals but not tested.

For 5k coverage, the question is not only whether the groups differ but whether they are close enough to count as equivalent. I therefore used two one-sided tests (TOST; Lakens, 2017) with a margin of ±2 percentage points. The margin is anchored in the coverage literature: the gap between the minimal (95%) and optimal (98%) thresholds is 3 points, and a group difference of under 2 points is smaller than that gap. The margin is lenient in one respect. Coverage is near the ceiling, with a standard deviation of about 1 point, so ±2 points is close to two standard deviations, and an individual text could still cross a threshold within it. The equivalence claim therefore concerns the group means on the raw scale, not every text. Equivalence is shown if the 90% confidence interval for the difference lies entirely within ±2 points.

**Robustness checks.** <!-- source: corpus.py length-matching window -->To rule out text length as an explanation, every measure was recomputed on a length-matched version of the coursebook texts: whole sentences from the start of each text until at least 360 words, close to the length of the examination passages. To check that pooling the June and resit papers, or adding new papers, did not drive the results, the primary tests were rerun with the June papers only (17 texts) and with the original 13. These checks are reported without correction.

**RQ2: what separates the groups.** To see which features distinguish the examination texts when all measures are considered together, I fitted an elastic-net logistic regression (Zou & Hastie, 2005; scikit-learn, Pedregosa et al., 2011) predicting group from the 21 open measures, excluding the two readability formulas. All measures were standardised. Performance was estimated with nested cross-validation. The outer loop left out one text at a time. <!-- source: separation.py settings -->The inner loop chose the penalty strength (nine values from 0.01 to 100) and the mix of lasso and ridge penalties (L1 ratio 0.1, 0.5 or 0.9) by stratified five-fold cross-validation. To test whether the separation exceeds chance, the whole nested procedure was rerun on 200 random permutations of the group labels. A measure counts as selected if the model keeps it in at least 80% of the outer folds. The model was fitted separately to the full and the length-matched coursebook texts.

**RQ2: genre.** Each text was assigned a genre label according to its dominant communicative purpose: fiction, personal narrative, biography, informational, argumentative, speech, review, or other (no text was finally labelled personal narrative). The labels were grouped into narrative and non-narrative. A feature article that opens with an anecdote and then explains or argues, for instance, is coded by what it mainly does. The coding scheme placed reviews and plot summaries under non-narrative. In confirming the labels I departed from it for two coursebook texts, a chapter-by-chapter plot retelling and a historical recount, both of which I coded as narrative (*other*). Under the scheme as written, the coursebook split would be 10 narrative and 17 non-narrative. The labels were first proposed with the help of an AI assistant (Claude, Anthropic), which gave a reason for each. I then confirmed or changed every label, and the confirmed set was frozen before any genre analysis. A second coder, blind to these labels and to the source of each text, independently coded a stratified sample of 20 texts that covered both groups and every label in use. Agreement was perfect on the narrative/non-narrative grouping (Cohen's κ = 1.00; 20 of 20 texts) and substantial on the fine labels (κ = .74; 16 of 20). Disagreements are reported in Appendix C and were not used to change the frozen labels.

I compared the genre composition of the two groups with Fisher's exact test on the narrative/non-narrative split. To ask whether genre accounts for the differences found under RQ1, I then repeated the comparison on the non-narrative texts only for concreteness and passives, the two differences that the earlier analysis had reported and that the RQ1 results confirmed (passives as a descriptive measure), with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the two. As a check on the raw scale, I also fitted ordinary least squares regressions (statsmodels; Seabold & Perktold, 2010) of each measure on group, with and without the narrative/non-narrative variable.

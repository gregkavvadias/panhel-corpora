Teachers and parents around the world have found themselves grappling with how to discuss major events with children - from mass protests and elections to wars and humanitarian crises. How can children be better prepared to understand and engage with the complex social, civic and political issues of today? In a world of misinformation, how can children be critically informed and prepared to participate in issues that affect their lives? These questions sit at the heart of civic education.

In its most basic form, civic education involves teaching about political institutions and processes, as well as citizens' rights and responsibilities. This includes understanding how government works, electoral processes, the rule of law, mechanisms of decision-making, and key social and political principles and values.

In today's complex world, however, many argue that contemporary civic education should go beyond this traditional model and include a broader range of competencies needed for effective citizenship. These include critical thinking and media and information literacy, which are essential for navigating an information-rich environment. Civic education can also develop skills for respectful dialogue in diverse societies and foster an understanding of global interconnections.

Schools remain among the institutions most responsive to public policy interventions. Over the past 50 years, an increasing number of countries have made civic education compulsory in public schools at primary, secondary or tertiary level. Yet the design, delivery and implementation of civic education vary significantly, reflecting different understandings of citizenship and political values.

In some countries, civic education exists as a distinct subject with dedicated curriculum time. In others, it is integrated across several subjects or treated as a cross-curricular theme. Some education systems emphasize formal classroom instruction, while others prioritize experiential learning through school activities and community engagement.

Research suggests that well-designed civic education can have significant positive effects. Studies commonly identify three main outcomes: students gain basic civic knowledge, become more politically active, and internalize deeper democratic values. The Committee on the Rights of the Child also stresses that civic education should support both present and future participation, helping adolescents take part meaningfully in decision-making now while building the capacities needed for lifelong civic engagement.
